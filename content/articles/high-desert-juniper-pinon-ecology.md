The scenery that most says 'New Mexico' isn't the dramatic dunes or towering peaks — it's the humble, sprawling piñon-juniper woodland that blankets the high desert. These gnarled, drought-hardened trees give the state its signature scent, its beloved nuts, and the very texture of its landscape.

## Survivors of the dry country

Piñon pines and junipers are masters of scarcity, thriving where little else can with deep roots and waxy, water-thrifty foliage. Slow-growing and long-lived, a twisted specimen may be centuries old.

> The smell of piñon smoke on a cold night is New Mexico distilled into a single breath.

## The taste and smell of place

Piñon nuts are a prized local harvest, gathered each fall and roasted, while burning piñon wood produces the sweet smoke that perfumes New Mexico evenings. Few sensory experiences are more tied to this place.

## A whole ecosystem

The woodland supports a rich web of life — birds, deer, and countless smaller creatures depend on its cover and its nuts. Drought and heat now threaten it, making its quiet endurance all the more precious.

**Field notes**

- Piñon nuts ripen in fall — a cherished local harvest.
- That sweet evening woodsmoke is burning piñon.
- Some twisted junipers are centuries old — tread gently.

Overlook it and you miss the true texture of New Mexico. The piñon-juniper woodland is the unglamorous, essential backdrop to nearly everything the state is — and one sniff of its smoke will call you back for life.
