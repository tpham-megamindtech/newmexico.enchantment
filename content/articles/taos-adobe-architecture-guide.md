Spend a day in Taos and the buildings start to speak. The rounded adobe walls, the wooden ladders leaned against upper stories, the roof beams punching through the plaster — none of it is decoration. It's a language of shelter refined over centuries in a harsh high-desert climate.

## Why the walls are round

Adobe — sun-dried mud and straw — has no hard edges to begin with, and its thick mass moderates temperature: cool in the searing afternoons, warm through the freezing nights. The soft, hand-smoothed corners are the material simply being itself.

> Adobe isn't a style New Mexico chose; it's the answer the desert gave.

## Vigas, latillas, and ladders

Those beam-ends jutting from the walls are vigas, the log rafters that carry the roof; between them run smaller latillas. The ladders, once the only way to reach upper rooms, survive as both tool and emblem.

## Old and new

Modern 'Pueblo Revival' buildings mimic the look with stucco over frame, but the real thing still stands all over Taos. Learn to tell them apart and every street becomes a reading lesson.

**Field notes**

- Many historic homes are private — admire from the street.
- The Taos Pueblo shows the tradition in its purest, oldest form.
- Golden-hour light makes the earth walls positively glow.

Once you can read it, you can't stop. The architecture of northern New Mexico turns an ordinary walk into a slow, satisfying act of translation.
