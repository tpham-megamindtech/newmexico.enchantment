Hang around New Mexico long enough and you'll notice them everywhere: long strings of deep-red chile pods dangling from portales, gateposts, and kitchen beams. The ristra is decoration, tradition, and pantry all at once — the state's most beautiful way of storing food.

## A pantry on a string

Before freezers, drying was how you kept chile through winter. Ripe red pods are tied into ristras and hung to cure in the dry desert air, ready to be broken off and simmered into red sauce whenever the pot calls.

> A ristra is a New Mexican kitchen wearing its winter supply on its sleeve.

## Luck and welcome

Beyond the practical, ristras carry meaning: hung by the door, they're said to bring good luck and signal welcome. Their glowing color against adobe walls has become one of the state's defining images.

## Buy a real one

Roadside stands and farm markets sell true edible ristras alongside purely decorative ones. If you plan to cook from yours, ask — and store it somewhere dry and airy.

**Field notes**

- Edible ristras should be kept dry and out of humidity.
- The reddest, glossiest pods make the best sauce.
- Many roadside ristras are decorative only — ask before cooking.

Equal parts larder and folk art, the ristra sums up the New Mexican relationship with chile: practical, beautiful, and impossible to separate from home.
