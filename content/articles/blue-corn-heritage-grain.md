Long before green chile, there was corn — and in New Mexico, often it was blue. Grown by Pueblo peoples for centuries, blue corn carries a nutty, earthy flavor and a deep cultural weight, turning up today in everything from tortillas to fine-dining tasting menus.

## A Pueblo staple

Blue corn has been cultivated in the Southwest for generations, ground on stone metates into meal for tortillas, atole, and the paper-thin piki bread of the Hopi and Pueblo kitchens. Its color comes from natural pigments that also make it nutritionally distinct.

> Blue corn tastes of the Southwest itself — patient, ancient, and rooted in this ground.

## Taste the difference

Compared with yellow corn, blue reads nuttier and slightly sweeter, with a faintly grainy texture. In a stack of blue corn pancakes with pine nuts and honey, or a plate of blue corn enchiladas, the flavor is unmistakable.

## Old grain, new menus

Contemporary New Mexican chefs have embraced blue corn as both heritage and hallmark, milling it locally and building dishes around its color and character. Ordering it is a small act of eating in place.

**Field notes**

- Blue corn pancakes with piñon are a classic Santa Fe breakfast.
- Look for locally milled meal at farmers markets.
- The color fades with heat but the flavor stays.

Eat blue corn in New Mexico and you're tasting a line that runs back centuries — a grain that fed this land long before it had a name on any map.
