Long before galleries lined Canyon Road, the Pueblo peoples of New Mexico were making some of North America's finest ceramics. Coiled by hand from local clay, painted with yucca brushes, and fired in open flame, Pueblo pottery is not a craft revival but an unbroken tradition — and learning to see it well changes how you understand the whole state.

## Every pueblo, its own style

The pottery of each pueblo has distinct hallmarks — the polished blackware of one, the intricate geometric painting of another, the storyteller figures of a third. Trained eyes can often name the origin of a pot at a glance.

> A Pueblo pot isn't decorated with tradition; it is tradition, coiled and fired by hand.

## Made the old way

The best pieces are still hand-coiled without a wheel, burnished with a smooth stone, and fired outdoors, where the flames leave marks no kiln could replicate. That labor is invisible until you know to look for it — and then it's everywhere.

## Buying with respect

Seek work sold directly by the artist or through reputable dealers who name the maker. A signed, sourced piece supports a living lineage; a cheap knockoff undermines it. Ask questions — genuine artists love to answer them.

**Field notes**

- Buy directly from artists at markets or the pueblos when you can.
- Ask about the maker, the clay, and the firing method.
- Hand-coiled, stone-polished pieces carry the deepest value.

Hold a genuine Pueblo pot and you hold centuries of accumulated knowledge in your hands. It is the most quietly profound art you can carry home from New Mexico.
