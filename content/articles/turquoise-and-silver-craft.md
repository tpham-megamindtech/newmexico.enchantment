Nothing signals the Southwest quite like turquoise set in silver. The jewelry traditions of the Navajo, Zuni, and Pueblo silversmiths transformed a blue-green stone into a globally recognized art form, and learning to read their work is one of the quiet pleasures of time spent in New Mexico.

## Distinct hands, distinct styles

Navajo work often features bold silver and large stones; Zuni artists are known for intricate inlay and 'needlepoint' clusters of tiny cut stones. Each tradition has its own vocabulary, and the differences reward a closer look.

> Turquoise isn't just set in silver here — it's set in generations of skill.

## Knowing your stone

Natural turquoise varies enormously by mine, from sky blue to deep green, often webbed with dark matrix. Much of what's sold cheaply is stabilized or imitation, so buying from reputable, transparent sources matters.

## Buy the story

A genuine, signed piece comes with provenance — the artist, sometimes the mine. That story is part of the value, and honest dealers and the artists themselves are glad to share it.

**Field notes**

- Learn the difference between Navajo and Zuni styles.
- Ask whether turquoise is natural, stabilized, or imitation.
- Buy signed work from artists or reputable dealers.

Once you can tell inlay from overlay and matrix from imitation, every silver-and-turquoise piece becomes a small lesson in craft. It's a stone, a metal, and centuries of mastery, worn on the wrist.
