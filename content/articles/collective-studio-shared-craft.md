Behind much of New Mexico's craft output stands an unglamorous hero: the shared studio. Artist collectives — pooling kilns, tools, and rent — have quietly become the backbone of the state's ceramic and craft scene, keeping the work affordable and the knowledge flowing between generations.

## Why collectives work

Kilns are expensive and studio space is scarce, so makers band together to share both. The economics keep emerging artists in the game, and the proximity means techniques and encouragement pass freely between members.

> A shared kiln fires more than pots — it forges a whole community of makers.

## Knowledge in the room

A newcomer struggling with a glaze can simply ask the veteran two benches over. That informal apprenticeship, more than any class, is how craft traditions actually survive and evolve.

## Open to visitors

Many collectives host open studios and sales, offering a rare window into working artists' spaces. It's the best way to buy directly, meet the makers, and see the community that produced the work.

**Field notes**

- Watch for open-studio days and collective sales.
- Buying here often means meeting the artist face to face.
- Collectives are where emerging talent is easiest to find.

They rarely make headlines, but the collectives are where New Mexico's craft future is quietly being fired. Support one and you're investing in the whole ecosystem.
