Some states have a bird and a flower and stop there. New Mexico went further and named an official state cookie: the biscochito, a buttery, anise-scented shortbread dusted in cinnamon sugar that appears at every wedding, holiday, and feast day across the state.

## A cookie with history

Brought by early Spanish settlers and adapted over centuries, the biscochito traditionally uses lard for its distinctive flaky crumb, with anise seed and a splash of brandy or wine. In 1989 the legislature made it the first official state cookie in the nation.

> One bite of a biscochito and a New Mexican is instantly back at their grandmother's table.

## Cut, bake, and dust

The dough is rolled and cut — often into fleur-de-lis shapes — baked until pale gold, then rolled warm in cinnamon sugar. The result is delicate, crumbly, and dangerously easy to eat by the dozen.

## The taste of celebration

Biscochitos mean something here. They signal Christmas, weddings, baptisms, and every gathering worth marking. Many families guard their recipe closely and bake enormous batches each December.

**Field notes**

- The traditional flavor comes from anise seed — don't skip it.
- They keep well, making them ideal holiday gifts.
- Look for them at bakeries year-round, not just in December.

Humble, fragrant, and woven into every celebration, the biscochito earns its official status the way all great regional foods do: by tasting unmistakably of home.
