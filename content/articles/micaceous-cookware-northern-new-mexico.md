Some of New Mexico's most distinctive pottery quite literally glitters. Made from clay naturally flecked with mica, the micaceous cookware of the northern pueblos and Hispanic villages shimmers gold in the light — and, remarkably, it's built not just to admire but to cook in.

## Clay that sparkles

The mica in the local clay gives these pots their signature glint and, crucially, a resistance to thermal shock that lets them go straight onto a flame. Beans cooked in a micaceous pot are said, by devotees, to taste unmistakably better.

> It's the rare art you're encouraged to put on the stove and cook dinner in.

## Cookware as heirloom

These pieces are passed down through families and used for generations. A well-seasoned bean pot is both a working tool and a treasured object, blurring the line between kitchenware and art.

## A northern specialty

The tradition is concentrated in the north, around Taos and the mountain villages, where the right clay is found. Buying from a maker there connects you to a very specific corner of the state's craft heritage.

**Field notes**

- Season a new pot before its first use, as the maker advises.
- Micaceous ware can go over direct flame — that's the point.
- Buy from northern pueblo and village artisans directly.

Equal parts cookware and craft, micaceous pottery embodies a very New Mexican idea: that the most beautiful objects are often the ones you actually use.
